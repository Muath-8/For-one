# For one: enjoyment and pull

Perspective written 2026-10-09 for Muath. One of eleven lenses on the For one concept. It builds on the first rethink ("For one: the next evolution, a rethink") and does not repeat it.

## Decisions only you can make

Three calls change what gets built. My recommendation comes first in each row.

| # | Decision | Recommended | Alternative | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Can a story continue across episodes? | Yes. Allow story threads (arcs) that run 1 to 3 weeks, while every episode still stands on its own. Plot may persist; obligation never does. | Keep Law 4: only memory persists, never plot | Serial stories bring people back and recycle vocabulary. A personal show can do this without punishing a missed day, which broadcast shows cannot. |
| 2 | When does the learner choose the episode? | At the end of today's session, for tomorrow. It then arrives at the time they picked. | Pitches shown when the app opens (current PRD) | Choosing the night before creates anticipation and removes the decision at the moment of starting, where friction costs the most (my inference). |
| 3 | What counts as success for pull? | "Worth it" and unprompted return, with time spent watched only as a warning light | Sessions and minutes per week (current PRD metrics) | The research shows hours track obsessive engagement better than healthy engagement. If minutes are the goal, the product will drift toward a feed. |

If you answer only one, answer #1. It decides whether the show is a set of one-offs or a series, and that shapes the planner, the content and the hosts.

## The short answer

Do not try to be as pulling as Instagram. Try to be as easy to start as Instagram and as anticipated as a favorite show's new episode.

Feeds pull through four things: a cue, zero friction to start, unpredictable rewards, and no point to stop. The first two are healthy and For one should copy them. The last two make people use more while enjoying it less, and the evidence on that is consistent. A good show pulls through different things: anticipation, characters you care about, a question you want answered, and the sense that it was worth your time.

My judgment, from the evidence below: For one's biggest risk on this lens is not a lack of hooks. It is sameness. AI-generated stories converge on the same plots, names and tone, and interest in AI conversation partners fades after the novelty. The current concept has strong rules against manipulation and almost nothing against monotony. The design work that matters most is a show with a world, a cast that stays the same people, stories that continue, and a system that keeps every week different.

Three changes to the concept follow:

1. **From episodes to a series.** Give each learner's show a persistent world and story arcs. This is also how vocabulary comes back without drills.
2. **From pitches at open to a choice the night before.** Close today by choosing tomorrow, and deliver it at the learner's chosen time.
3. **From "no manipulation" to "built against monotony too."** Add a variety system next to the trust contract: real-world input, a ban list for stock tropes, rotation of formats, and a check that episodes are not all alike.

## What makes media pull people

"Strong" means a meta-analysis or a well-replicated result. "Moderate" means one good study or a large field trial. "Weak" means small, self-reported or industry data.

### Feeds and habit

| Finding | Strength | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wanting and liking come from separate brain systems. Cues can make people want something more without enjoying it more. | Strong in animals and pharmacology; an analogy for apps | Berridge 2018 |
| Instagram users post sooner after getting more likes, the way animals learn from rewards. Gaps between posts were about 18% shorter at high reward rates. | Moderate to strong (1M posts plus an experiment) | Lindström et al. 2021 |
| Once a habit forms, rewards stop mattering. Heavy posters kept posting whether or not they got reactions; the cue carries them. | Moderate | Anderson & Wood 2023 |
| Platform revenue tracks how many people use it several times a day, r = 0.71. Cues tied to events in the day built habit better than reminders. | Moderate | Anderson & Wood 2021 |
| Turning autoplay off cut Netflix viewing by about 18 minutes per session in a randomized test. | Moderate (n = 76) | Schaffner et al. 2025 |
| Recommendation feeds and autoplay most undermine people's sense of control on YouTube. | Moderate | Lukoff et al. 2021 |

| --- | --- | --- |

### What healthy pull looks like

| Finding | Strength | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In games, feeling free to choose and feeling competent predicted enjoyment and wanting to play again. | Moderate, widely built on | Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006 |
| Harmonious passion goes with positive feeling (r = .35) and flow (.43). Obsessive passion goes with rumination (.47), dependence (.56) and more hours (.19). | Strong (94 studies) | Curran et al. 2015 |

That second row is the most useful number in this section. Time spent is a better marker of the unhealthy kind of engagement than of the healthy kind.

### Stories, curiosity, humor and learning

| Finding | Strength | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |

## Borrow, adapt or refuse

My judgment on each pull mechanism, using the evidence above.

| Mechanism | What it does in media | Verdict | For one's version |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A cue at a fixed moment | Builds automatic habit | Borrow | The episode arrives at a time and place the learner chose, inside a routine they already have. |
| Zero friction to start | Removes the decision to begin | Borrow | Tomorrow's episode is chosen today, so opening the app means pressing play. |
| Anticipation | Waiting for something good is pleasant | Borrow | One new episode a day, previewed the night before. Drip, never binge. |
| Characters you know | Podcast loyalty is mostly host loyalty | Borrow | One stable duo with long memory. Their voice and personality never change silently. |
| Serial story | Brings people back to find out what happens | Adapt | Arcs that run 1 to 3 weeks, but the story waits for the learner. Nothing happens while they are away. |
| Curiosity gap | A question you half know the answer to | Borrow | The argument thrown to the learner is pitched at about 50% certainty, and the call settles it. |
| Humor and surprise | Captures attention | Borrow | Running jokes and callbacks, with the target words inside the joke. |
| Variable reward | Unpredictable payoffs keep people checking | Adapt | Surprise lives in the content (a callback from three weeks ago, a guest, a twist), never in prizes for showing up. |
| Personalized feed | Endless supply matched to taste | Refuse the endless part | Personal content, finite amount. One episode, a small bounded extra at most. |
| Autoplay and infinite scroll | Removes the moment to stop | Refuse | Every session ends with a clear sign-off. No "next" button. |
| Streaks and loss aversion | People protect what they might lose | Refuse | A missed day costs nothing; the show picks up where the learner is. |
| Cliffhanger sold per episode | Pocket FM's model | Refuse | Never charge for, or withhold, the end of a story. |
| Guilt on leaving | "Don't go yet" messages | Refuse | Hosts end conversations cleanly. 37% of companion-app goodbyes use these tactics, and users find them creepy. |

The line I would draw: **pull is healthy when it comes from what the learner gets, and unhealthy when it comes from what they fear losing or cannot stop.**

## What AI-made personal content changes

AI changes the equation in four ways. Two help For one and two threaten it.

**It helps: content can be about you.** Self-reference (effect about .45) and interest-based personalization are among the best-supported levers in learning, and no broadcast show can use them. This is For one's real edge over Netflix and podcasts: the story is about the learner's team, job and Friday interview.

**It helps: a story can wait for you.** A broadcast serial runs on the calendar, so missing it means falling behind. A personal serial runs on the learner's time. If they skip three days, the hosts pick up where they left off. This makes serial pull possible without fear of missing out. As far as I know, no media has had this property before.

**It threatens: sameness.** The evidence here is converging:

- AI ideas made stories more alike (10.7% more similar) even as each one improved (Doshi & Hauser 2024).
- Across 20,000 AI stories, 11 words appeared in 88% of them, including the names Elias, Mara and Elara (Hamilton & Mimno 2026, preprint).
- Stories from 10 different models converged on one generic plot, and changing temperature or prompts did not fix it (DK & Hatzel 2026, preprint).
- Non-experts rate AI stories as more creative than expert judges do (Ismayilzada et al., preprint). So an AI judge or a first-day user will overrate quality.

A show that is fresh on day 1 and predictable by day 20 is exactly how novelty fades. Fryer et al. (2017) found students' interest in a chatbot partner dropped after the first task, while interest in a human partner did not. Those were pre-LLM chatbots, and no good multi-week LLM study exists yet. My inference is that the risk remains.

**It threatens: the AI penalty.** People rate identical writing about 6% lower when told AI wrote it (Berg et al. 2026, 16 experiments). 47% of US podcast listeners say they would be less likely to keep listening if a favorite show introduced AI voices (Sounds Profitable 2025). For one cannot hide that it is AI and should not try. So the show must win on what a human show cannot offer, which is relevance to one person, not on general polish.

**A warning from the market.** Pocket FM shows where infinite AI supply goes when pull is the only goal. Its users average about 150 minutes a day on serials with thousands of cliffhanger episodes, sold per episode. AI cut its production cost from about $2,000 to $30 per content hour. Forbes calls the output "a lot more slop," and its CEO says much of the category relies on dark patterns (Forbes 2026). That is the version to avoid.

**Regulation now covers recurring AI hosts.** California's SB 243 (in force since 1 January 2026) covers any AI that meets social needs and "sustains a relationship across multiple interactions." A recurring host you call plausibly qualifies. That means clear AI disclosure, a published self-harm protocol, and annual reporting from July 2027, with $1,000 per violation in private suits. The EU has also made preliminary findings against TikTok (February 2026) and Meta (July 2026) for infinite scroll, autoplay and push notifications. This is my reading, not legal advice, and should be checked with a lawyer before launch.

## The content and experience design this implies

### 1. A show with a world, not a stream of episodes

Each learner's show gets a series bible: the two hosts, three to five recurring side characters (a host's neighbor, the learner's colleague as they described them), recurring places and running jokes. Episodes happen in that world. This is what makes it a show you know.

It also solves a learning problem the rethink raised. Words come back because the world comes back. Watching one series needs fewer distinct words and repeats rare ones more, and each repeat raises the odds of learning by about 25%. The planner can do on purpose what TV series do by accident: thread each new word through the world 10 or more times over two weeks.

### 2. Three layers of story

| Layer | Length | What it is | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Episode | One session | A complete question, settled in the call | "Was the referee right?" |
| Arc | 1 to 3 weeks | A thread that continues and resolves | The learner's job interview: prep, the day itself, the debrief |
| Season | 4 to 6 weeks | A shape with an ending | Ends with a finale episode that doubles as the speaking sample and the proof |

Rules for arcs:
- Every episode satisfies on its own. An arc adds a reason to come back, never a requirement.
- No more than two arcs open at once.
- An arc waits for the learner. If they are away, nothing happens without them, and the hosts pick up the thread with one line.
- The best arcs come from the learner's real life (the interview, the trip, the move), which also keeps the show pointing outward.

### 3. The day, redesigned for ease of starting

1. **The night before, at the end of the chat:** the host offers tomorrow's 3 options. The learner picks one or invents their own. That is the only decision of the day.
2. **At the chosen time:** the episode arrives, in their podcast app or as one quiet message. Opening it means pressing play.
3. **The episode:** a curiosity question pitched at about 50% certainty, a running joke, one callback the learner did not expect.
4. **The call and the chat:** the learner settles the question. The answer carries the target words.
5. **The ending:** a clear sign-off, then tomorrow's choice. No "next episode" button and no extra feed.

### 4. Surprise in the content, never in rewards

Media keeps people checking with unpredictable rewards. For one's version is earned surprise inside the show: a host remembers something the learner said three weeks ago, a side character returns, a guest appears, the format changes for one day. Nothing is a prize for showing up, and nothing is lost by staying away.

### 5. A variety system to fight sameness

My judgment, informed by the homogeneity research:

- **Real input every day.** Each episode must use at least one thing that did not come from the model: the learner's life, today's news on their interests, their own words from the last call.
- **A trope and name ban list,** updated as patterns show up. Stock names, lighthouse settings and "unexpected friendship" plots go on it.
- **Format rotation.** Debate, story, mystery, interview, gossip, explain-it. Track the last 10 episodes and never repeat a format three times in a row.
- **A similarity check across a learner's episodes,** run in code like the coverage check. If this episode is too close to the last 20, regenerate it.
- **Authored hosts.** The default duo is written by people, as the PRD says. Humans write the characters; the model writes the episodes.

### 6. Hosts as show hosts, not companions

The relationship is the strongest pull For one has. It also carries the most risk.

- **Continuity is a promise.** Never change a host's voice, personality or memory silently. If a change is needed, the host explains it on air.
- **Warmth about the content and the learner's life,** never about the host's own need for the learner.
- **Bounded voice time.** Keep the call cap. Look for heavy-use outliers and, if they appear, have the show point them outward (real conversation partners, real situations).
- **Build SB 243 compliance in from the start:** AI disclosure, a self-harm protocol, reporting.

### 7. Choices in the right amount

Two to four small choices per session, each with three to five options: tomorrow's topic, which host leads, how an arc goes. Character creation and invented formats stay available but are not the daily path. Whatever the learner shapes must show up, finished, in a later episode, because people only value what they made when it is completed.

## How to know the pull is healthy

The current PRD measures sessions, completion and retention. Keep them, and add measures of whether the time was worth it. All of these are my proposals.

| Measure | What it tells you | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unprompted return | Pull without nagging | Share of sessions started without any cue, and sessions started within an hour of the chosen drop time |
| Tomorrow chosen | Anticipation | Share of sessions that end with tomorrow's episode picked |
| Worth it | Liking, not just wanting | A one-tap question asked rarely (for example, once a week in the proof episode), or asked by the host in conversation |
| Regret | The unhealthy kind of pull | The same question, asked about the last week |
| Passion type | Harmonious or obsessive engagement | The standard passion scale in a beta survey at week 4 |
| Sameness | Whether episodes stay fresh | Similarity score across each learner's episodes, plus a monthly human read of 10 random episodes per learner |
| Heavy use | Early warning | Learners well above the median minutes a day, flagged for review |
| Blind preference | The 8am test, for real | In Milestone 0, have learners rate a For one episode against a human-made learner podcast on the same topic |

For Milestone 0, I would add one question to the rethink's gate: in week 2, are learners still choosing tomorrow's episode, and do they say the last week was worth it? A show that is loved on day 1 and dutiful by day 14 is the failure this lens is most worried about.

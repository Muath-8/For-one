# For one: keeping a learner for a year without games

*Perspective: the psychology of consistency and motivation. Written 2026-10-09 for Muath. Builds on the rethink doc (https://claude.ai/code/artifact/21c354e1-9b24-4fcd-bb44-dca39f26d86b) and does not repeat it. Published doc: https://claude.ai/code/artifact/5d21e0ee-dd03-4ae9-a35c-2b4e49b20924*

For one's consistency problem is not motivation on day 1. It is the two to five months before a routine runs by itself, and the first lapse, which the current design has no answer for.

## Decisions only you can make

Four calls change what gets built. My recommendation is first in each row.

| # | Decision | Recommended | Alternative | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Weekly rhythm | The learner picks a weekly rhythm with spare days ("4 mornings a week, 2 spare"). The show remembers it and talks about it in the weekly proof. It is never shown as a counter, calendar or flame. | No frequency goal at all | Goals with built-in spare days beat both easy and strict goals. Rigid daily rules backfire. With no goal, the learner has no idea what "on track" means. |
| 2 | When a learner goes quiet | Opt-in. At onboarding the learner writes one short note to their future self, sent once after a gap they choose (for example 10 days). | Never contact (the current PRD) | Time since the last visit is the strongest predictor of not coming back. Manipulative nudges backfire, but a note the learner wrote is their own plan, not a trick. |
| 3 | Seasons | The show runs in 6 to 8 week seasons. Each aims at one real goal the learner names and ends with a finale: voice then and now, plus a real-world try. | One endless show | Near goals beat distant ones. A season break is a natural fresh start and a chance to refresh the format before novelty fades. |
| 4 | Story continuity | Light, optional threads (a host's ongoing life) that reward coming back but never require earlier episodes | Strictly self-contained, no plot (current law 4) | Curiosity pulls people back and improves memory, but obligation and backlog create guilt. Optional threads keep the pull without the debt. This one is my judgment; the evidence is indirect. |

This lens also strengthens two of the six calls already in the rethink: #4 (honest, non-game progress) and #5 (one cue the learner designs). I would treat both as a yes.

## The verdict

**Wanting to learn is rarely what runs out.** Motivation researchers separate deciding to learn from keeping going (Dörnyei & Ottó). Most people who quit language apps did not stop wanting to speak; they lost the routine. In a 6-month study of 601 Duolingo beginners, motivation did not predict who stayed once persistence and age were counted.

**What keeps people going for a year is a routine with a cue, a forgiving rhythm, a way back after a lapse, and competence they can feel.** Streaks and points are one way to supply the first two, and to be fair, they work for daily return: Duolingo reports 58.7M daily users, a DAU/MAU of about 42% and 84% next-day return among current users. Their cost is that they turn a missed day into a loss. When a streak breaks, people continue less than if there had been no streak at all, and showing them their record makes it worse.

**For one already gets the quality of motivation right**: choice, no guilt, honesty, a relationship, real proof. What it lacks is the scaffolding of consistency. There is no cue, no rhythm, no plan for a lapse, no arc longer than a week, and the strongest lever it has, the weekly proof, is withheld from exactly the people who are slipping.

## What the research says

"Strong" means a meta-analysis or replicated finding. "Moderate" means one good field study or experiment. "Weak" means small, unreplicated or contested. "Company-reported" means an unaudited claim. Everything in the right-hand column is my inference.

### How habits form

| Finding | Strength | What it means for For one |
|---|---|---|
| A habit is a response to a context cue, not a decision. Changing intentions changes occasional behavior but not habitual behavior (Wood & Rünger 2016, citing Webb & Sheeran's 47-study meta-analysis). | Strong | The design target is a fixed moment in the day, not more motivation. Motivational messages do not build habits. |
| Reaching automaticity took 66 days on average, range 18 to 254 (Lally et al. 2010, n = 82). A 2024 review: medians 59 to 66 days, means 106 to 154, individual range 4 to 335 (Singh et al. 2024, only 4 studies). | Moderate | Expect 2 to 5 months of support before the routine runs itself. The current design has no cue in that window. |
| Gym habits took months; handwashing took weeks. For 76% of gym members the strongest predictor of going was time since the last visit; the same weekday mattered for 69%; time of day did not matter (Buyalskaya et al. 2023, ~30,000 members). | Moderate to strong | Absence compounds. The days right after a gap are when to act, and the PRD does nothing until the learner happens to open the app. |
| Missing one day did not hurt habit formation; many misses lowered the ceiling (Lally et al. 2010). | Moderate | A forgiving rhythm is fully compatible with forming a habit. A consecutive-day count is not needed. |
| Paying people to exercise in a fixed daily window produced fewer gym visits than paying for any time, during and after the program (Beshears et al. 2021). | Moderate | Encourage a regular moment. Never make it a rule. |
| Flossing right after brushing built stronger habits at 8 months than flossing before it (Judah et al. 2013, n = 50). | Weak to moderate | Anchor the session to something the learner already does every day. |
| Moving house breaks habits and opens a window of about 3 months for new ones (Verplanken & Roy 2016; Wood & Rünger 2016). | Moderate | Life changes will break the For one habit too. Detect them and re-anchor. They are also the best time to start (a new job, a coming trip). |
| Spaced practice beats massed practice, and the best spacing grows with how long you need to remember (Cepeda et al. 2006). | Strong | Short, regular sessions over a year beat bursts. The 2-a-day cap is right. |

### Plans and goals

| Finding | Strength | What it means for For one |
|---|---|---|
| If-then plans ("when X, I will Y") help: d = .65 across 94 tests (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006). A newer meta-analysis of the related WOOP method finds g = .34, or .24 after correcting for publication bias (Wang et al. 2021). In 250 online courses, planning prompts helped early, then faded (Kizilcec et al. 2020). | Strong that they help; small at scale | Capture a when-where-backup plan at onboarding, but do not expect one prompt to carry a year. Revisit the plan when it fails. |
| Specific goals beat "do your best" (d = .42 to .80). On complex new skills, learning goals beat performance goals (Locke & Latham 2002). | Strong | Goals should be about what you will be able to do ("follow the derby commentary"), never scores or minutes. |
| Near subgoals beat one distant goal on persistence and self-efficacy (Bandura & Schunk 1981). | Weak to moderate | "Fluent someday" motivates nothing week to week. Break the year into near goals. |
| Goals with 2 emergency skips met their target up to 40% more often than easy or strict goals. The day after a miss, 55% of the reserve group got back on track versus 37% of the strict group (Sharif & Shu 2017, 2021). | Moderate | Build spare days into the rhythm. This is the evidence-based replacement for a streak. |
| The "fresh start effect": gym visits rise at the start of a week (+33%), a month (+14%) and a semester (+47%) (Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014). | Moderate, correlational | Time returns and season starts to natural landmarks: a Monday, a new month, a birthday, a new job. |
| People speed up as a goal nears, even when the head start is illusory (Kivetz et al. 2006). | Moderate to strong | A season finale gives a real goal gradient made of real progress, with no points needed. |

### What rewards, choice and game elements do

| Finding | Strength | What it means for For one |
|---|---|---|
| Expected tangible rewards lower intrinsic motivation (d = −.36). Positive verbal feedback raises it (d = +.33; +.43 for adults) (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, 128 experiments). | Strong, size debated | Never pay learners in tokens. Do tell them, specifically, what they did well. A host that never praises gives up a proven lever. |
| Feedback motivates when it informs and demotivates when it feels controlling. Feeling competent only helps alongside feeling autonomous (Ryan & Deci 2000). | Strong | Progress is shown as information the learner owns, never as a target someone else set. |
| Choice raises intrinsic motivation (d ≈ .36). Three to five options per choice did slightly best, though not robustly (Patall et al. 2008). | Strong for choice | The PRD's 3 to 5 pitches are well supported. Keep them. |
| Gamified learning: cognitive g = .49, motivational .36, behavioral .25, but in the most rigorous studies only the cognitive effect holds (Sailer & Homner 2020). | Strong meta-analysis; motivational effect fragile | Game elements do not reliably build motivation, so leaving them out costs less than it seems. |
| Points, levels and leaderboards raised the quantity of work, not its quality or intrinsic motivation (Mekler et al. 2017). | Moderate | Game elements buy more taps, not better learning. |
| A gamified platform's effect began fading around week 4 and only partly recovered (Rodrigues et al. 2022, 756 students). A badge-and-leaderboard course lowered motivation and exam scores over a semester (Hanus & Fox 2015). | Moderate; weak to moderate | Any novelty, including the novelty of AI, is a month-one asset. Something must renew interest after it fades. |

### Streaks and real drop-off

| Finding | Strength | What it means for For one |
|---|---|---|
| Duolingo, Q2 2026: 58.7M daily and 140.6M monthly users (DAU/MAU about 42%), 84% next-day return among current users. At the end of 2024, 10M+ people had year-long streaks. Learners with a 7-day streak are 3.6x more likely to finish a course. Streak Revival brought back about 8M lapsed users. | Company-reported | Streaks work for daily return at scale. Going without one is a real bet, so measure against this bar. |
| After a streak breaks, people continue less: 78% versus 53% in a language-lesson study. Showing the activity log helped intact streaks but hurt broken ones (45% with the log versus 61% without). Self-blamed breaks were worst (29%). Making the streak repairable recovered most of the drop (85% versus 69%) (Silverman & Barasch 2023). | Moderate | Never show a record of missed days. When someone returns, treat the gap as life, not character, and as fully repairable. |
| Education apps keep about 18% of users on day 1, 8 to 10% on day 7 and 4% on day 30 (Adjust 2023). Mental-health apps: 3.3% median at day 30 (Baumel et al. 2019). edX course completion: about 3% (Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente 2019). | Company-reported; moderate; strong descriptive | The default is near-total loss within a month. The first weeks are the cliff. |
| In 601 Duolingo beginners followed for 6 months, persistence of effort and older age predicted staying; motivation did not survive in the model (Sudina, Teimouri & Plonsky 2025). | Moderate | Wanting to learn is not what keeps people. Routine and self-regulation are. |
| I found no peer-reviewed public figure for 6- or 12-month retention in language apps. | Gap | Your beta will produce the first real numbers. Instrument it from day one. |

### Lapses and coming back

| Finding | Strength | What it means for For one |
|---|---|---|
| Of 54 gym programs tested on 61,293 people, the best gave small rewards for returning after a missed workout. Only 8% of programs still had an effect after they ended, and experts could not predict the winners (Milkman et al. 2021). | Strong, short-term | Design the return, not just the routine. Test mechanics in the beta instead of trusting intuition, including mine. |
| Most smoking lapses are small, and reactions to the first lapse did not predict relapse; lower confidence after a lapse sped up the next one (Kirchner et al. 2012). | Moderate | After a gap, rebuild confidence fast with an easy, real win. |
| Self-compassion after a failure led people to study longer for a retest (Breines & Chen 2012). | Weak to moderate | After a gap the host is warm and matter-of-fact. No guilt, no "we missed you". |
| 37% of AI-companion goodbyes use manipulation. It raises engagement up to 14x, and also anger, intent to quit and perceived manipulation (De Freitas et al. 2025). | Moderate, working paper | The PRD's ban on "I miss you" tricks is right and should stay. |

### Language learning specifically

| Finding | Strength | What it means for For one |
|---|---|---|
| A vivid "ideal self" as a speaker correlates .61 with intended effort but only .20 with achievement (Al-Hoorie 2018, N = 32,078). The measure may really capture self-confidence (Al-Hoorie et al. 2024). | Strong; construct contested | A vivid goal raises intention, not results. Vision alone is cheap. |
| Visualization alone did not raise willingness to speak; visualization plus goal setting did (Munezane 2015). | Weak to moderate | Pair "future you" with concrete near goals, which is what seasons do. |
| Anxiety correlates −.36 with achievement (Teimouri et al. 2019; Zhang 2019, −.34). Enjoyment correlates .30 with achievement and .48 with willingness to speak (Botes et al. 2022). | Strong, correlational | The live call is both the best and the scariest part. Its first weeks need an on-ramp. |
| Self-efficacy correlates .46 with L2 achievement (Goetze & Driver 2022). Past successes are the strongest source of self-efficacy (Usher & Pajares 2008; Byars-Winston et al. 2017). | Strong | Real-world wins are the most powerful motivator available. "Life is the game" should be a mechanic, not a slogan. |
| Motivation differs by stage: choosing to start, keeping going, then judging the outcome (Dörnyei & Ottó 1998). Long motivational surges share a vision, a launch, routines with subgoals and visible progress checks (Dörnyei, Henry & Muir 2016). | Theory; weak evidence | Different months need different mechanics. |
| The "intermediate plateau" is widely reported by teachers, but I found no solid research on it. | Weak | Treat it as a design hypothesis: plan for months 3 to 6 to feel flat. |

### Relationship and pull

| Finding | Strength | What it means for For one |
|---|---|---|
| Listeners bond with podcast hosts. When hosts or content change, "it used to be good" reviews appear (8.8% of 12,100 reviews) (Vilceanu 2025). No study shows these bonds cause retention. | Weak to moderate | Keep hosts consistent, and do not rely on the bond alone. |
| People value what they built (+63%), but only when it is finished (Norton, Mochon & Ariely 2012). | Moderate | Shaping a host makes it theirs, if the creation is short and completes. |
| Curiosity improves memory (Kang et al. 2009, small samples; Gruber et al. 2014). | Moderate | An open question is a reason to come back. "Never plot" gives this up. |
| Audiobooks only at the gym raised visits 51%, then faded (Milkman et al. 2014). At scale, the effect was 10 to 14% and lasted up to 17 weeks (Kirgios et al. 2020). | Moderate | Put the episode inside a routine the learner already enjoys: the commute, their podcast app, the morning coffee. |

## How the current concept holds up

| Part of the concept | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The learner picks topic, format and cast from 3 to 5 pitches | Supported | Choice raises intrinsic motivation (Patall); autonomy is a core need (Ryan & Deci). |
| No guilt, no "I miss you", honest about being AI | Supported | Manipulation raises quitting (De Freitas); controlling pressure lowers motivation (Ryan & Deci). |
| Weekly proof with real deltas and honest flat weeks | Supported: the strongest lever | Mastery experience is the top source of self-efficacy; informational feedback raises motivation. |
| Self-contained sessions, nothing to fall behind on | Supported | A backlog invites self-blame, and self-blamed breaks hurt most (Silverman & Barasch). |
| Two sessions a day at most | Supported | Spaced practice beats massed practice (Cepeda). |
| Difficulty eases on low-energy days | Supported | Anxiety tracks lower achievement; challenge matched to skill supports flow. |
| "The show is the gym, life is the game" | Supported, but not built | Real successes are the strongest motivator, and the PRD has no mechanic for real-world tries beyond follow-up seeds. |
| Hosts never change silently | Supported | Host changes trigger "it used to be good" (Vilceanu). |
| No notifications at all | Contradicted | Habits run on cues, and time since the last visit predicts dropping out. |
| No goals beyond the intake "why" | Missing | Specific near-term learning goals beat vague distant ones (Locke & Latham; Bandura & Schunk). |
| Weekly proof only in weeks with 2+ sessions | Works against its own purpose | The learners who are slipping lose the strongest lever exactly when they need it. |
| Hosts never praise | Partly contradicted | Per-turn praise is rightly banned, but specific positive feedback raises intrinsic motivation (d = +.33). |
| Nothing happens when a learner disappears | Missing | The best of 54 tested programs rewarded the return (Milkman). |
| No plan for life changes (a move, a trip, a new job) | Missing | Context changes break habits (Verplanken & Roy). |
| A live call-in on day 1 | Risky | Anxiety is the main threat to speaking; the voice-note mode exists only as a failure fallback. |
| Success metrics stop at week 4 | Missing | Nothing measures return after a lapse or retention at months 3, 6 and 12. |

## The year, phase by phase

Each stretch of the year fails for a different reason, and a lapse can come in any of them. The return episode is the piece most worth building first: a lapse happens in every phase, and the current design has nothing for it.

| Phase | What usually goes wrong | What carries the learner |
|---|---|---|
| Start, days 1 to 14 | Choice motivation is high, but the first live call can scare, and there is no routine yet. | An anchor plan at onboarding. Voice notes before the first live call. The day-2 callback (already in the PRD). One specific win named in week 1. |
| Habit forming, weeks 3 to 10 | Novelty fades around week 4, the first lapse arrives, and automaticity is still about two months away. | A weekly rhythm with spare days. A small door for bad days. A return episode after gaps. The first season finale at week 6 to 8. |
| The flat middle, months 3 to 6 | Progress gets harder to feel, and episodes start to feel alike. | Season 2 with a new real goal. Then-and-now over 3 months, not 1 week. A first real clip they now understand. New formats or a guest host. |
| Life happens, months 6 to 12 | Moves, trips and new jobs break the cue. A reached goal raises "what now?" | Re-anchor when life changes. Pause without penalty. Bigger real-world goals. A year-in-review episode. Later, human bridges. |

## The mechanics

### 1. The anchor: one moment, chosen by the learner

- During onboarding the producer asks three things: what you already do every day that this could follow, where you will be, and what you will do if that moment fails. It is stored as an if-then plan: "After I sit down on the 7:40 bus, I play my episode. If I miss the bus, I listen at lunch."
- The episode arrives at that moment, once, as the rethink's decision #5 describes (one learner-designed cue, or a private podcast feed).
- The plan is revisited, not repeated. If the learner misses the anchor two weeks running, or mentions a move, a new job or a trip, the producer asks for a new one.
- Evidence: habits run on context cues (Wood & Rünger), anchoring to an existing routine helps (Judah), if-then plans help (Gollwitzer & Sheeran), and one-off prompts fade at scale, hence the revisit (Kizilcec).

### 2. The rhythm: a weekly intention with spare days, never a streak

- The learner sets a weekly rhythm, for example "4 days, with 2 spare". Spare days are called spare days, not misses.
- It is never shown as a counter, calendar or flame. The show refers to it in words, in the weekly proof: "Four times this week, as you planned."
- The record shows what was gained, never which days were missed.
- The unit is the week, not the day. That matches the weekly proof and the weekday regularity that predicted gym habits.
- Evidence: emergency reserves (Sharif & Shu), a single miss does not hurt habit formation (Lally), flexibility beats rigid timing (Beshears), and a missed-days record hurts after a break (Silverman & Barasch).

### 3. The small door: a session that fits a bad day

- Every day offers a 3-minute version: the hosts' 90-second recap of the episode and one voice note back. It counts fully toward the weekly rhythm.
- The habit is the link between the cue and starting. Protecting that link on bad days matters more than the dose.
- Evidence: repetition in a stable context builds habit (Lally), and time since the last visit predicts dropping out (Buyalskaya). The 3-minute size is my judgment. It fits the rethink's "one episode, many doors".

### 4. The return: what happens after a gap

- If the learner opted in, one note arrives after the gap they chose, in their own words, written at onboarding: "It's you from October. Lisbon is in March." It is sent once, with no follow-ups, and no host sends it.
- The first session back is a return episode. It is one step easier, opens from something in their life ("how did the move go?"), never mentions how many days were missed, and gives a quick real win, such as something they can now say that they could not say in week one.
- The gap is treated as life, not character. Self-blamed breaks are the most damaging (Silverman & Barasch), and self-compassion helps people try again (Breines & Chen).
- When a natural landmark is near (a Monday, the start of a month or a new season), the show offers the fresh start (Dai, Milkman & Riis).
- The weekly proof still arrives in a slow week. It is shorter and about what has built up over time, not about the week.
- Evidence: rewarding the return was the best of 54 programs (Milkman), confidence after a lapse predicts the next lapse (Kirchner), and manipulation backfires (De Freitas).

### 5. The arc: seasons with a real goal

- A season lasts 6 to 8 weeks. At its start the learner names one real-world goal in plain words: "order and chat at a café in Lisbon in May", "follow the derby commentary". It is a learning goal, never a score.
- Episodes thread toward it invisibly. Midway there is a rehearsal of the real scene. At the end comes a finale episode: their voice then and now, the real-world attempt and a debrief.
- Between seasons there is a short break and a fresh start: a new goal, and perhaps a new format or a guest host, which counters the week-4 novelty fade.
- The next season's goal is set inside the finale, so reaching a goal does not become a reason to stop. This is my judgment.
- Evidence: near goals (Bandura & Schunk), learning goals (Locke & Latham), vision plus goals (Munezane), goal gradient (Kivetz), fresh starts (Dai) and the structure of long motivational surges (Dörnyei, Henry & Muir). TV seasons are a borrowed format, which fits law 6.

### 6. The evidence: competence the learner can hear

The rethink's honest progress (decision #4) covers the basics. This lens adds four things.

- **Specific praise, rarely.** The host names one real thing: "you just argued for four minutes in the past tense." It is never per-turn and never generic.
- **Real-world tries.** The learner chooses them ("want to order in Spanish on Saturday?"), and the next session opens with how it went. They are offered, never assigned, which keeps law 8.
- **Longer windows as the year goes on.** Then-and-now comparisons at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months and 1 year. Weekly gains are hard to hear in the flat middle; 3-month gaps are not.
- **Graduation moments.** Once a season, a real clip from a real show, with how much of it the learner now understands. This is my judgment.

### 7. The pull: reasons to come back that are not games

- Optional threads, as in decision #4: a host's sister's wedding, an ongoing argument about a team. They reward returning but are never needed to follow an episode.
- Put the episode where the learner already has a pleasant routine: the commute, their podcast app, the morning coffee (temptation bundling).
- Never silently change a host's voice or personality.
- Keep host shaping short and finished. People value what they built only when it is complete.
- Talk to the learner as a speaker, not a student. When something is hard, say why it matters: "this is the stuff real speakers use." Identity framing of difficulty has moderate support (Oyserman). Wording tricks like "be a speaker" do not: the famous "be a voter" result failed to replicate.

## What to measure

The PRD's beta metrics stop at week 4 and count sessions. Add these:

- **Weeks on rhythm**: the share of weeks the learner met their own chosen rhythm. Count weeks, not days.
- **Return rate**: the share of learners who come back within 14 days of a gap of 7 days or more, and the median gap length.
- **Anchor stability**: the share of sessions that happen at the learner's anchor moment, as a habit proxy. A short monthly self-report automaticity question (the 4-item Self-Report Behavioural Automaticity Index) would confirm it; I did not re-check that instrument in this research.
- **Retention at weeks 4, 12, 26 and 52**, compared with the 4% day-30 baseline for education apps and Duolingo's 42% DAU/MAU as the gamified bar.
- **Seasons completed and real-world tries attempted.**
- **A learning outcome** (the rethink's 4-week speaking sample), so retention never becomes the only goal.

## Where the evidence ends and my judgment begins

**Backed by evidence:** habits need stable cues; forgiving goals beat strict ones; token rewards undermine motivation while specific feedback helps it; streak breaks hurt and records of misses make it worse; anxiety and self-efficacy matter a great deal in language learning; choice helps; rewarding the return works.

**My judgment:** the specific mechanics (the 3-minute small door, 6 to 8 week seasons, the learner-written note, optional threads, graduation clips); that leaving out streaks costs less than it seems for adults who chose a personal show; and that the novelty of AI fades the way game novelty does. None of these has been tested in a language app. Experts could not predict which gym programs would work (Milkman), so the beta should test the return episode, the opt-in note and the weekly rhythm against simpler versions.

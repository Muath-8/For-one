For one, in detail

The long version of the overview. It covers what each part does, the rules it follows and the numbers behind them. Open any grey bar to see more.

Source: the product requirements (PRD-v3), the decision records, the technical design and the backlog. Where documents disagree, the later decision wins (ADR-25 over ADR-24). Ids such as FR-325 point to the exact requirement if you ever want the original wording.

What it is

One account is one private show: a daily episode, a call with its host, an article, a short chat, and a teacher behind it that plans each day from what you did yesterday.

The learner is any adult (18 or older) who already understands part of a language, roughly A2 to C1, and wants to speak it. The design centre is the person who understands a lot and freezes when speaking. The product is a web app that can be installed on a phone's home screen, plus a private podcast feed. Nothing else is planned: no native app, no Apple or Google sign-in.

Everything an episode contains is made for one person from what that person said. Nothing is graded on screen, nobody is told they are wrong, and no game mechanic exists anywhere.

The eight trust promises
  • Never lie. A memory is only stated if confirmed; a progress claim only if it passed the gate.
  • Never manipulate. No guilt, dark patterns or "don't go" goodbyes. Exactly three kinds of message are ever sent.
  • Never hide what it is. The hosts and Salma say they are AI at the start and whenever asked.
  • Prove progress honestly, including flat and worse weeks.
  • The data is theirs: see it, export it, delete it.
  • Told, not inferred. The show uses only what the learner said to it. Nothing is looked up about them, nothing is guessed about health, money, relationships, religion, politics or sexuality.
  • A wrong memory is a bug. Fixed immediately, and counted as a quality measure (target: fewer than 1 callback in 100 wrong).
  • Data use is opt-in. De-identified transcripts help improve the teacher only if the learner switches it on. Off by default.

The first ten minutes

Sound inside a minute, speech inside five, and the account only after the first call. Ten steps, labelled A1 to A10 in the documents.

  1. A1 OpenOne screen in the learner's own language: "Your own show, in English." A Start button and an "I'm 18 or older" checkbox. Nothing else.
  2. A2 MicrophoneSalma explains she will talk for about 3 minutes. Buttons: Talk (asks for the mic only now) or Type. A line under it says Salma is an AI and that voice is not kept unless the learner makes an account.
  3. A3 Salma talksHer voice starts within 60 seconds of opening. She asks three things: what the hosts should call you (said aloud so the hosts learn the pronunciation), what you could talk about for an hour, and what you need the language for. Each fact appears as a card with edit and remove. Twice she switches to English for one line: a comprehension check and an invitation to say anything back. At most 8 turns; a question skipped after one re-ask is left blank.
  4. A4 Word check (optional)"Tap the words you know." 48 items: 36 real words from six frequency bands plus 12 invented words. Stops at 75 seconds. Skip is as big as Start.
  5. A5 CastingWhile the first episode is being made (40 to 90 seconds) each host says hello. The learner can swap to another voice from the pool and pick an accent where there is more than one.
  6. A6 The first episodeA 2 to 3 minute episode about the topic they gave, using their name once. The transcript is shown the first time, with a hint about "Huh?" and tapping words. Starts playing within 45 seconds of the end of A3.
  7. A7 First call60 to 90 seconds. A host asks a question answerable in a sentence or two. Before it rings, the other host explains the word button. No recasts in this call; the host only asks for clarification if meaning is lost.
  8. A8 One thing managedIn the goodbye the host names one real thing the learner did that was not easy ("you used should have"). Nothing about what went wrong.
  9. A9 Keep your showAccount by emailed one-time code, birth date (under 18 is refused), the drop time in life terms ("on my commute, 7:40"), a voice-storage choice, and one required consent to process what they say in calls (it can touch health, beliefs or relationships). The 7-day trial starts here on the 2-a-day plan with no card.
  10. A10 TomorrowThree ideas for tomorrow plus "Tell Salma an idea". The learner picks one, sees "Tomorrow at 7:40", and gets a one-time card to add the app to the home screen and the show to a podcast app.
Details worth knowing
  • The first episode counts as day 1's episode. The first three episodes are pitched one step easy and treated as calibration.
  • The word check and the first call give a starting estimate for listening, reading, speaking and writing, each with a wide range.
  • In Arabic, where "you" is gendered, A3 offers an optional chip for masculine, feminine or neutral wording. Default is neutral; it is never guessed.
  • Salma speaks in the learner's language; at A3 the default is that at least 80% of her words are in that language during season 1.
  • Day 2's episode must include a callback taken word for word from something said in onboarding.
  • Week 1 asks at most one personal question per session, in a fixed order: weekly rhythm, then the first then-and-now recording, then the season goal, then the optional note to the future self, then people and past attempts.

After launch (not in the first release): a landing page that lets visitors make a personalized 90-second sample episode before signing up. At launch the landing page is plain: one sentence and a "Start your show" button.

The daily loop

Choose tonight, it is made overnight, it arrives at a time you picked, and nothing else ever nudges you.

Choosing tomorrow

  • At the end of the last thing you do today, and on the Today screen once the session is done, the app shows three ideas, each one line of at most 140 characters, with topic, format and cast ("Mona interviews you for the job, and she is not nice.").
  • Or "Tell Salma an idea": type or say what you want. Salma confirms it in one line and it becomes the pick.
  • Rules for the three: at least one from the learner's own life if something is due; exactly one wildcard outside their usual interests; at most one continues an open story; no format that already ran twice in a row; no real-world news stated as fact unless the learner supplied it.
  • Remix: long-press an idea to change its topic, format or cast.
  • Cutoff: if nothing is chosen 6 hours before the drop time, the teacher's top-ranked idea is used.
  • If today brings news that changes an idea (the interview moved), the teacher rewrites that idea before showing it.

Making and dropping

  • When the pick is made the teacher writes the brief and the pipeline runs on cheaper batch pricing. If it is not finished 90 minutes before the drop time, the remaining steps switch to standard on-demand pricing. The pipeline never fails to deliver: on any failure a fallback episode ships.
  • The episode appears in the app as soon as it is ready. The drop time controls the private feed and the notification, not access.
  • At the drop time: the episode is published to the private feed and one notification fires (web push, if installed and allowed). The title and length only, nothing else.
  • The feed version adds a 5-second tag from the host at the end: "To call me, open the link in the notes". The notes carry the call and article links and a "Heard it" link. Feed URLs contain an unguessable token, are kept out of podcast directories, and can be reset.

Gaps, bad days and rhythm

The small door

Every episode ships with a 90-second recap by the hosts ending in one question. On a bad day, play it and record one voice note (up to 60 seconds). A host answers in one short text. It counts as an active day.

Return episode

After 5 or more days with no session, the next episode is one step easier, opens from something in the learner's life, never mentions the days missed, and includes one quick real win from checked evidence.

Gap note (optional)

Offered once in week 1. The learner writes a note to their future self and picks a gap of 7, 10 or 14 days. If that many days pass, the note is sent once by email and push, in their own words. Then nothing more.

Anchor and rhythm

The anchor is the drop time in life terms ("after I sit down on the 7:40 bus"). The rhythm is 3 to 6 days a week plus 2 spare days. It is never shown as a counter, calendar or flame. Salma offers a new anchor if no session happens near the anchor for two weeks.

Every message that leaves the product

Exactly three kinds, and a unit test fixes the count:

  1. The drop notification at the drop time, only when a new episode dropped. Can be turned off.
  2. The gap note, once, opt-in.
  3. Transactional email: sign-in code, receipts, trial ending (day 6), payment failed, export ready, account deleted.

No re-engagement, no "your hosts miss you", no streak warnings, no marketing email to learners. Only one module in the code is allowed to send email or push.

Inside a session

A session is one episode and everything attached to it. Each part is a door: Listen, Read, Call, Chat, Round two, Small door. Doors open in any order, with a few sensible limits.

  • Chat opens after Listen, Read or Call has been used. Round two opens after the main call.
  • The episode ends by throwing to the learner. The screen offers Call now, Read the article first, or Later. Reading first is suggested because the article is planning time.
  • If the article is skipped, a 30-second planning card shows three words the learner might want. Skippable.
  • Position persists: audio, article scroll, chat history. A call cannot resume: an interrupted call restarts as a new call whose first line acknowledges the gap, never blaming the learner.
  • Skips are allowed and logged as signals. Nothing punishes a skip.
  • One session is open at a time. Starting the next episode closes the old one with no confirmation; nothing is lost.

Read: the article

150 to 300 words at A2, 250 to 450 at B1 and B2, 350 to 600 at C1 and above. It must add something the episode did not (a counterargument, a missed fact, or a reason to switch sides), never a recap. A host header says "This is what I'll throw at you on the call." At least 98% of the words are known. "Read it to me" plays it aloud, synced. Every word is tappable.

Call: the live call

Entered with one tap from the throw, from Today or from the feed link. The screen stays awake. Earphones are suggested on the first call. The call needs the screen; there is no lock-screen call.

The word button

Hold the button and say a word in your own language. The audio goes to a recognizer for your language, not the host's. The host waits, then gives the word in English inside the conversation ("A selection? Of what?") and carries on. The word also shows in a small bubble.

If you have never produced the word, you get it. If you have produced it before, you get a hint first (its first sound, or "you used it on Tuesday about your manager") and the word on a second press. Using the button less over the weeks counts as progress evidence. Salma stays silent in calls; the host says the word so the call has one voice.

Anxiety mode, captions, failure, recording
  • Anxiety mode starts if your median turn in the first two minutes is under 4 words, or you have two 8-second silences, or your last call was abandoned. For the rest of the call: at most one recall gap, fewer recasts, more backchannel, simpler questions.
  • Captions: the host's words are captioned by default (off in Controls). Your own words are never captioned live. No red, no ticks, no score on the call screen.
  • If live voice fails: "Weak signal. Let's do voice notes." The same conversation continues as voice notes in the call thread. The session is never lost.
  • Recording: the live transcript runs the conversation. After the call the audio is re-transcribed word for word. With voice storage on, audio is kept encrypted. With it off, audio is deleted when re-transcription succeeds, and always within 15 minutes of the call ending. Transcripts are always stored.
  • Round two after launch: one short call per episode, 2 to 3 minutes: "Give me your take again, shorter and faster."

After the call

There is nothing to read. Today shows one honest line, such as "6:12 call, you spoke 3:40". No notes, no polish card, no list of mistakes from anyone. Behind the scenes the after-session pass logs what you said and the important errors become material for the next plan: the right forms come back in later episodes and calls. A first-ever success that passed the claim gate may be mentioned by a host in the next episode's opening, at most one per episode. A word you reached for and did not have becomes a planted word in the next episode.

Chat

  • One long thread per host across days. The host's first message arrives within 30 seconds of opening or of the call ending.
  • The opener asks you to restate one point first, before adding anything new. The host sends 3 to 5 messages and ends at message 5 with a natural closer, then tomorrow's three ideas appear. If you keep writing, one final short reply, then quiet.
  • No margin note. At most once per chat the host may say your point back naturally ("So the meeting was cancelled? Classic."). If you ask how to write something, you get a hint first, not a rewrite.
  • Text only in v1. Each bubble sets its own text direction.

The word sheet

Tap a word to see its meaning in your language for this sense, the sentence it came from, a button to hear it (cut from the host audio), and Keep this word. Meanings come from a shared cache keyed by word, sense and native language, all prepared before the episode ships, so the sheet opens in under 300 ms. Keeping a word adds it to the notebook and tells the teacher to bring it back. A word tapped twice or more becomes a recall candidate.

Hosts, Salma and seasons

Karim and Mona

The English host duo, shared by every native language that learns English. Each has a written "bible": values, opinions, humour, how they disagree, how they treat the learner, what they never do, and their in-show storylines. People write the bibles; the AI only writes episodes. In the call you talk to one host, the one who threw to you.

Salma

One producer per native language. Her voice is closer and drier than the hosts'. On screen she is a monogram, not a face. She does onboarding, life updates, "tell Salma an idea", the notebook, settings by conversation, "what do you know about me?", fact corrections, "where do I stand?", and "why do we say it like that?" when asked. She never corrects, sends notes, or performs in the show.

Voices

Three candidate voices per host per accent, chosen by a blind listening test. The learner can swap within the pool. A voice swap or rename is announced on air in one line in the next episode. A host's personality never changes silently, even across a model or vendor upgrade.

Salma's language balance

In season 1 at least 80% of Salma's words are in the learner's own language. Each new season her English share rises by about 10 points if the speaking estimate rose, never above 70%. The learner can ask for more or less at any time.

The series: world, arcs, seasons

  • Each learner has a series bible: authored hosts, 3 to 5 side characters made by the writer (a character based on a real person uses only what the learner said), places, running jokes, open arcs, the season goal, and the show canon (positions already taken, things already said), which blocks repeated angles and contradictions.
  • Arcs last 1 to 3 weeks, at most two open at once, best taken from the learner's real life (an interview: prep, the day, the debrief). An arc waits for the learner; nothing happens while they are away. Every episode in an arc stands alone.
  • Seasons are 4 weeks and cannot be broken: a season ends on its date whether the learner did 3 episodes or 25. Each season has one real goal in the learner's words, a learning goal and never a score. Salma names it from what happened ("Season 2: Interview season").
  • Mid-season rehearsal: if the goal is a real scene (an interview, a call), one episode uses the rehearsal format. The call is the scene itself, with the host playing the other person.

Honesty and crisis

  • Salma says she is AI in her first lines and the hosts in theirs. Portraits carry a small "AI" mark. Every character answers "are you real?" honestly, every time.
  • The hosts' storylines are the show's fiction, like a sitcom. A host never claims to exist outside the show, to remember you personally between episodes, or to miss you.
  • Crisis protocol: every live role and the analyst watch for self-harm or crisis. In a call, a keyword waits up to 2 seconds for the host's confirmation, then the protocol fires anyway (a missed crisis costs more than a false alarm). The host stops, and a pre-recorded clip in the learner's language, in Salma's voice, says plainly that this is an AI and cannot help with this. The screen then shows helplines for the learner's country from a maintained international directory. The session continues only if the learner chooses.
  • Each activation is logged without content (date, country, resource shown, whether the call ended). Muath is told only the weekly count.

How an episode is made

A durable chain of steps runs overnight. Each step has a timeout and retries.

  1. Brief (teacher)Idea, format, cast, arc and season context, one grammar target, 2 to 4 due or new words, coverage targets, length, speech rate, up to two usable life facts, the callback, the learner's energy and anxiety state, and the "why this episode" reasons.
  2. WriterThe script with scene breaks and speaker turns, the article, the call brief, the chat opener, the 90-second recap and two shadow lines.
  3. FitterCode splits the text into words and phrases and gives each a probability that this learner understands it. Expected coverage is the average. Default pass: 95% for audio, 98% for text. Only failing sentences are rewritten, at most two rounds. New words must stay within budget, and every target and due word must appear where planned.
  4. EditorEleven pass/fail checks (below), run by a model from a different company than the writer, plus code.
  5. Voice studioTwo-voice audio with expressive tags, the article read-aloud, the recap, the feed tag, word timestamps, and the learner's name pronounced from their own recording.
  6. Audio re-listenThe rendered audio is run back through speech recognition and compared with the script. Lines that are skipped, garbled, or mispronounce the learner's name or a target word are re-recorded up to twice, then rewritten with a synonym or shipped with that line marked.
  7. Gloss precomputeMeanings for every word in the script and article, only for words not already in the shared cache.
  8. PublishTo the app immediately; to the private feed at the drop time.

Variety and sameness defences
  • Real input in every episode: at least one element that did not come from the model: the learner's life, their words from the last call, or something they told the show this week.
  • Ban list: stock names, settings and plots AI stories converge on, kept per language and updated monthly.
  • Format rotation over the last 10 episodes, no format three times in a row. Formats: debate, story time, news-style rundown (of the learner's own news), interview, explain-it-to-me, mystery, gossip, roast, rehearsal. A learner can describe any other format to Salma.
  • Similarity check: each script is compared with the last 20 scripts; above a cosine threshold of 0.85 it is regenerated.
  • Surprise in the content, never in rewards: a callback from weeks ago, a returning side character, a one-day format change.
  • Review read: an AI review pass, separate from the editor, reads 10 random episodes per learner per month for errors, sameness and law-breaking. Muath reads what it flags.
  • Shared once for everyone: the word-meaning cache, the host and Salma bibles, the ban list and the sound design.

The learner model

An append-only log is the truth. Everything else is calculated from it and can be rebuilt.

  • The event log: every transcript, tap, replay, skip, timing and edit is appended and never changed. Each event stores the learner, session, door, type, time, payload, the model and prompt versions that produced it, and its cost. When the analyst improves, the whole model is rebuilt by re-running the after-session pass over the log. Claims already shown are never silently retracted.
  • Models propose, code decides. Every model output is a candidate update in a fixed schema that code validates and applies.
  • Live roles never write. The host and Salma only produce transcripts. Only the after-session roles write to the model.
  • Every estimate carries a range.

Life-fact rules

  1. Quote check: a fact is stored only if its source quote appears word for word in a verbatim transcript or chat.
  2. Confidence ladder: said once means stored but never spoken, shown in the notebook as "I think...". Said again in another session, confirmed when asked, or left unedited on a notebook card the learner viewed means usable. Edited by the learner means ground truth at once.
  3. Sensitive facts (health, money, relationships, religion, politics, sexuality) are used only in the session where the learner raised them. They are stored and used later only after a one-tap confirmation card in the notebook. A spoken "yes" is not enough. Never brought up first.
  4. Time: each fact type has a shelf life (job: months; "sick this week": days). Expired facts are never spoken. A conflicting new fact replaces the old and keeps history. A dated event triggers one follow-up after its date.
  5. Interests decay over a few weeks without engagement. Dormant interests are never pitched but wake the moment the learner mentions them.
  6. Corrections: when you correct the show, it says "My mistake, noted." (the one allowed use of that word), fixes the fact at once, and logs a wrong-memory flag.

The teacher

An AI planner that never speaks. It decides what tomorrow teaches and why.

When it runs

  • After each session, once the after-session pass finishes (within 10 minutes).
  • Nightly in batch: the weekly mix, arc and season progress, and tomorrow's three ideas.
  • When a dated follow-up comes due, after a gap of 5+ days (return episode), and at a season boundary. Overlapping triggers merge into one run.
  • If it fails, a rules-based brief is built from the due list, the top interest and the next format in rotation. If the model itself is unavailable, the last good plan is used. The learner always gets an episode.

Choosing targets

Candidate grammar structures and words are scored on six factors: need (how often it goes wrong and how much it hurts understanding), goal fit, readiness, learnability, opportunity (can tomorrow's episode make it natural and necessary) and due (about to be forgotten).

  • Caps: 1 grammar target and 2 to 4 words per session; no target in more than 4 sessions in a row. Where you confuse two forms, both are targeted in the same episode so both are needed.
  • An error pattern enters targeting only after 3 observations across at least 2 sessions.
  • The "working on" list shows at most 3 items in plain words. You can veto one (it leaves targeting unless it breaks meaning) or add one ("I want to sound professional on calls"), and the teacher acts on it the same day.
Treatment by learner state
If you...The teacher
never attempted it but are expected to by nowInput flood: hosts use it often and audibly for 1 to 2 weeks, no correction, before it becomes a speaking target

Review schedule, new words, difficulty
  • Review: every word, phrase and structure has a spaced-repetition state (FSRS-style, in code). It is "due" when predicted recall falls below 0.85 by the next drop time. Grammar targets repeat every 1 to 3 days early on; words on widening gaps.
  • Due items return through four channels: the same day across surfaces; across days in new stories; through the recurring world (arcs, running jokes); and through hosts' "you mean..." lines that echo your sentences.
  • New words: 3 to 6 per episode, at least 80% from the top 3,000 most common words.
  • The edge: audio coverage default 95% (easier 96%, harder 94%, floor 93%); text default 98% (99%, 97%, floor 96%); speech rate steps of 5%; length default 6 minutes (plus or minus 1, cap 8).
  • One step easier for: the first three episodes, a return episode, low-energy signals (short replies, a skipped call, an error spike, "today was too hard"), or a Huh? rate half again above your 2-week median. One step harder after two sessions in a row with a low Huh? rate, full uptake and no low-energy signal. At most one step per session.
  • Ideas are ranked by predicted choice: interest salience times format history times arc pull times topic recency.
  • "Why this episode": 2 to 4 plain reasons per episode, each traceable to the model ("You said on Monday the derby was Sunday."). Never a comment on days away.

Assessment and proof

Two standards: planning signals can be fast and noisy, because a wrong guess costs one slightly mistargeted episode. Claims to the learner must be slow and checked, because one false claim costs trust in every later one. Nothing in assessment runs during the live call.

ClockWhat runsMay influence
During the callNothing is gradedThe conversation only
After each session (10 min)The after-session passTomorrow's plan, never a claim
WeeklyFluency and complexity on retell turns, smoothed over 2 weeks; avoidance checkDifficulty; the proof only through the gate
Every 4 weeksThe then-and-now recording, rated by a calibrated model plus code metricsSpeaking claims; skill estimates

The after-session pass

  1. Verbatim re-transcriptionEvery learner audio turn, keeping fillers, false starts and self-corrections, with word timings and confidences. The live transcript is never used for assessment, because live recognizers silently fix learner errors.
  2. AnalystA small model with a fixed schema produces, for each turn, the smallest correction that makes it acceptable, a comprehension rating of 0 to 2, can-do moments, words reached for, whether you repeated a recast, and self-corrections.
  3. Edit classifier (code)Aligns original and corrected text and sorts each edit into the structure inventory and twelve reporting categories (verb tense, agreement, articles, prepositions, word order, word choice, plurals, pronouns, comparatives, conditionals, question formation, register).
  4. Confidence filterEdits inside speech the recognizer was unsure about are dropped and can never feed a claim.
  5. Measures and updater (code)Bayesian updates of skills, word probabilities and structure states, each with a range. Review schedules and target lifecycles move.
  6. Memory keeperA small model plus rules proposes life-fact candidates with quotes, interests, goals and show canon. Code validates them.
What is measured
  • Accuracy: correct use in obligatory contexts per structure; errors per 100 words by category, always read next to what was attempted.
  • Complexity and fluency: clause length, lexical diversity, share of uncommon words; speech rate, run length, mid-clause pauses. Measured only on retell turns, round two and the 4-weekly recording, so like is compared with like.
  • Vocabulary and comprehension: word probabilities and "words owned"; content uptake 0 to 2; Huh? rate; taps; pronunciation scores on known sentences only (internal, never shown); word-button use.
How a grammar structure becomes "learned"

Not seen, then Heard, then Attempting (used at least once), then Active target (chosen by the teacher), then Taking (correct and unprompted in 2 different sessions, at least one a day or more after it was last targeted), then Stable (about 80% correct over 14+ days across at least 5 contexts, and confirmed in the next 4-weekly recording). Rechecked at 2, 4 and 8 weeks; a failed recheck returns it to Active.

"Learned" in any claim means correct, unprompted, at least a day later, in a different session. Words owned means a word used correctly and unprompted in two different sessions at least a day apart.

The claim gate

Every sentence about progress, in the proof, the finale, a host line or "where do I stand", must pass five rules in code:

  1. Like for like: the same task type.
  2. Beyond the noise: the change exceeds about twice the measure's standard error.
  3. Repeated: at least two data points agree (first-ever events excepted).
  4. Clean evidence: grammar claims only from chat text or high-confidence speech.
  5. Openable: each claim carries its evidence and the learner can open "how we know".
At launch only two kinds of claim can pass: first-ever events ("you used should have for the first time") and words owned. Fluency and accuracy claims need a hand-tagged calibration set that is built after launch from opted-in data. When nothing passes, the app says so honestly ("too early to tell"). The proof producer only ever receives passed claims, so fabricated wins are impossible by construction.

Weekly proof, then-and-now, finale

The weekly proof

About 2 minutes, presented by Salma in your language, with your own clips and at most one host line. Sits above today's episode and in the feed. In order: one headline claim with "how we know"; the rhythm in words only if it was met; the next focus; the working-on list; and one question, "Was this week worth your time?". A slow week gets about 45 seconds about what has built up over time. A flat week says so and names the one thing being changed. A worse week says so with the likeliest reason.

Then-and-now recording

About 3 minutes of your speech, always the same kinds of task: a 60 to 90 second retell, a 60 to 90 second opinion, and 8 sentences to repeat. Nothing is scored on screen. The first "then" is offered after session 2, then once every season. Skippable; skipping means no voice comparison that month.

Season finale after launch

Offered at the first session on or after day 28: the new recording played next to the last, at most two measured differences in plain words, the real-world attempt if there was one, a short recap (episodes, hours heard, minutes spoken, words owned), and next season's goal.

"Where do I stand?"

Only when asked. Salma answers per skill with a range and what it means in real situations ("listening around B1: you can follow a colleague who speaks clearly"), and only for skills whose calibration is met. Otherwise she says plainly she cannot say reliably yet. CEFR words never appear unprompted.

Languages

Everything language-specific is configuration, not code. The two sides are separate, so any native language pairs with any target.

Target language: what you learn

One package per target holds host bibles and voices, the grammar structure inventory, edit-classification rules, the twelve reporting categories, level descriptors, the calibration set, the word-frequency list and lemmatizer, word-check items, the then-and-now task bank, the ban list and the sound design. Launch: English only, with British and American accents.

Native language: the app and Salma

One package per native language holds Salma's bible and voice, the interface text and text direction, meaning and caption prompts, the crisis clip, and localized category names. Launch: Arabic and English (English is the universal fallback).

The language gate: five checks to open a target

  1. Voices: Muath and native-speaking volunteers rate two-host episodes from the candidate vendors blind, and the winner is one most would listen to.
  2. Learner speech: on learner clips the verbatim transcript must keep the learner's errors instead of fixing them, and catch words said in the learner's own language.
  3. Writing: an AI review of 10 scripts against the laws and ban lists, then a fluent speaker finds them natural and correct.
  4. The call: the host answers within about a second on that language's speech stack.
  5. Structures: a grammar structure inventory exists.

A target that fails any check ships later, never with a weaker bar. A native language ships when Salma's voice passes the blind test with native volunteers, its interface text passes a native speaker's check of 50 items, and its recognizer catches words said with the word button. The testing order for later targets is an estimate, not a result: Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Japanese, Korean, Mandarin, Arabic, Hindi, Turkish, Russian. A language whose "worth it" answers fall well behind English closes to new learners until fixed.

English grammar inventory and word lists

The grammar inventory is built from open sources with ids styled after the English Grammar Profile (a few hundred structures from A2 to B2). If a licence for the Profile ever arrives, it is mapped onto those ids. The word list uses wordfreq. The recognizer test uses an open learner-speech corpus (speechocean762, Apache-2.0) plus about 30 volunteer clips with a one-line consent each.

How one day flows through the system
  1. Learner calls POST /v1/ideas/choose in the evening. The API emits idea.chosen to Inngest.
  2. Workflows run teacher, writer, fitter, editor and voice (batch). The episode is ready before the drop time.
  3. At the drop time the feed item is published and one web push is sent.
  4. The app loads GET /v1/today, plays the episode and sends event batches to POST /v1/events.
  5. POST /v1/calls returns a room URL and token. The browser joins the LiveKit room over WebRTC.
  6. The call worker streams speech recognition, the host model and voice per turn (about 1 second) and posts call events to the API.
  7. Each learner turn's audio goes to workflows for verbatim re-transcription.
  8. When the call ends, session/after-pass runs: analyst, measures, memory keeper, updater and claim gate, within 10 minutes. The teacher then plans tomorrow.

Rules the engineering follows

  • Every vendor behind an adapter; swapping is a config change. Two voice vendors are wired from the start. The writer and editor are never the same vendor.
  • Every model call is traced with role, vendor, model, prompt version, learner-model version, units and cost.
  • Paid side effects are idempotent: each pipeline step keys its vendor call by episode, step and attempt and reuses a stored result on retry.
  • Latency budgets are tests: a 50-call bench and a 30-clip recognizer test run in CI against staging; the host-reply median gate is 1.5 s.
  • No agent framework in production: a durable workflow engine calling model APIs directly.
  • Nothing per-country: time zones, scripts, voices, consent text and crisis resources come from the language packages and the learner's row.
  • Deploys are timed per learner: a release goes out only when under 2% of learners have a drop in the next 45 minutes.

Scope and how it is built

In version 1

Onboarding with Salma, the daily loop, the player, article, call, chat and word sheet, the private feed, the notebook and controls, the simple weekly proof, the claim gate with event claims only, the crisis protocol, plans and Paddle checkout, privacy features (export, deletion, voice-storage choice), cost tracing, evals.

Version 1.1, after launch

The Show archive tab, round two, shadowing, the season finale, the tape, the sample episode on the landing page, metric claims once a calibration set exists, own failed-payment handling, a second language through the gate, the observer role as the first addition.

Not planned

Native apps, Sign in with Apple or Google, character creation and guest hosts, a news scout, cloned learner voice, yearly plans, regional prices, referrals or community, employer or university seats, playback above 1.0x, any chart, percentage or level.

How the build is run

  • An AI coding agent builds it one story at a time under Muath: 16 epics and about 164 stories, in six phases ordered by dependency (foundation; pipeline and a minimal call; onboarding, Today and the feed; the full call and the after-session pass; the first week's surfaces; production and launch). No calendar dates.
  • For each story, the acceptance criteria are written as failing automated tests first, then the smallest code that passes. One story per branch and pull request.
  • An independent AI review reads every pull request; a separate security review also reads anything touching auth, keys, media URLs, the feed or deletion.
  • Muath approves: every merge to main, every database migration, every change to a prompt, rule file, bible, ban list, consent text, crisis resource or legal document, every change to vendor or price files, every production deploy, every new dependency that sends data to a new processor, and every change to the agent's own instructions.
  • The agent never holds a staging or production secret and never reads learner content. It works with synthetic learners and fakes.
  • When the documents do not answer a question, the agent stops and asks instead of inventing an answer.
  • Launch happens once, publicly, with open signup, when every v1 story passes and the launch checklist is green. Health measures are read 4 and 8 weeks later.

What only Muath can do before launch

Open the vendor and Paddle accounts, choose the domain, record the volunteer speech clips, read the English bibles and Salma's Arabic, complete the self-review, run the voice and recognizer tests (or rate them), and appoint an EU and UK representative if the company is outside them.
